Why We Soak Walnuts and Almonds — And What the Research Actually Says

Soaking nuts overnight is a traditional practice found across Iranian, Indian, and Middle Eastern food cultures. The benefits are real — but they are not all equal, and some claims about soaking are better supported than others. Here is an honest look at what the science actually shows.

7 min read Nutrition · Traditional Preparation · Gut Health

In our kitchen we soak walnuts and almonds overnight before eating them. This is not a wellness trend we picked up recently. Soaking nuts before eating them is a long-standing practice in Iranian food culture and across much of the Middle East, India, and traditional food communities worldwide. The soaked walnut alongside cheese, bread, and fresh herbs is a standard part of the Iranian breakfast table. Nobody needed a study to tell them it tasted better and felt easier to digest.

But because this is a site that tries to separate what is well-supported from what is marketing, it is worth being honest about what the research actually shows — including where the benefits are clear and where they are less certain than some wellness sources suggest.

What nuts contain that soaking addresses

Walnuts and almonds, like all nuts and seeds, contain compounds the plant produces to protect itself. These compounds are not harmful in typical dietary amounts, but they can reduce how much nutrition you extract from the nut and affect how easy it is to digest.

Phytic acid

Phytic acid is a compound found in most plant foods including nuts, grains, and legumes. It can bind to minerals like iron, zinc, calcium, and magnesium, forming complexes that the body cannot easily absorb. This is sometimes described as making the minerals unavailable. The effect is real but should be kept in proportion — phytic acid is one factor among many that affect mineral absorption, and most people eating a varied diet are not significantly affected by the phytic acid in a handful of nuts.

It is also worth noting that phytic acid is not purely a negative. It has antioxidant properties and has been studied for potential protective effects against kidney stones and certain cancers. The anti-nutrient framing captures only part of the picture.

Enzyme inhibitors

Raw nuts contain enzyme inhibitors that can interfere with the digestive enzymes that break down food. For people who notice digestive discomfort after eating raw nuts — bloating, heaviness, difficulty digesting — enzyme inhibitors are the likely culprit. Soaking reduces these inhibitors, which is why soaked nuts consistently feel easier to digest for most people.

Tannins

Tannins are the compounds responsible for the bitter, slightly astringent taste of raw walnuts in particular. They are concentrated in the papery brown skin. When you soak walnuts overnight the water turns brown — that is the tannins leaching out. The result is a noticeably milder, slightly sweeter walnut that is much more palatable, especially for children. This is arguably the most immediately noticeable benefit of soaking and the one that requires no scientific qualification — you can taste the difference.

What soaking actually does

Soaking does three things that are well-supported by both research and practical experience:

  • Reduces tannins — the water pulls tannins out of the skin, reducing bitterness significantly. For walnuts this is dramatic and immediately obvious in the taste.
  • Reduces enzyme inhibitors — water deactivates a meaningful portion of the enzyme inhibitors present in raw nuts. This is why soaked nuts feel easier to digest.
  • Initiates germination signals — soaking tells the nut that conditions are right to grow. This triggers enzymatic activity within the nut that begins breaking down some of its protective compounds.

Where the evidence is more nuanced

The phytic acid reduction from soaking nuts is real but smaller than many wellness sources suggest. A 2020 study found that soaking almonds for 12 hours did modestly increase calcium and iron bioaccessibility — but the difference was small. For walnuts specifically, the same study found that soaking for 12 hours slightly decreased calcium and zinc content, though again by a very small amount. The phytic acid reduction benefit for nuts is significantly smaller than for grains and legumes, where soaking makes a much more meaningful difference. If you soak nuts primarily for the mineral absorption benefit, manage your expectations. If you soak them because they taste better and feel easier to digest — both of those benefits are real and consistent.

Walnuts and almonds specifically

Walnuts

Soak overnight in filtered water — 6 to 12 hours

Walnuts benefit most visibly from soaking because of their high tannin content. The bitterness reduction is significant and immediately noticeable. After soaking overnight in filtered water, drain and rinse thoroughly. The soaked walnut has a softer, milder flavor that children and adults who find raw walnuts too bitter tend to enjoy much more.

Keep the papery brown skin on — it contains ellagic acid, a polyphenol with anti-inflammatory research behind it, and removing it reduces the nutritional value of the walnut.

Store soaked walnuts in the fridge and use within 2 to 3 days. They do not keep as long as raw walnuts once soaked.

Almonds

Soak overnight in filtered water — 8 to 12 hours

Almonds benefit from soaking for slightly different reasons. The almond skin contains tannins that inhibit mineral absorption, and soaking softens the skin significantly — after soaking, the skin slips off easily if you choose to remove it. Removing the skin after soaking eliminates a significant portion of both the tannins and the phytic acid and gives you a softer, creamier almond with better mineral bioaccessibility.

The trade-off: the almond skin also contains flavonoids with antioxidant value. Removing it means losing some of that. For most people, particularly those with lower iron or zinc status, the mineral absorption benefit is worth it. For those eating a varied, nutrient-rich diet, keeping the skin and simply soaking without peeling is a reasonable middle ground.

Store soaked almonds in the fridge and use within 2 days.

An important note — roasted nuts cannot be activated

Soaking only works on raw, unroasted nuts. Heat destroys the enzymatic activity that makes soaking effective. A roasted almond or walnut has already had its biological potential deactivated by heat. You can soak a roasted nut for 24 hours and nothing meaningful will happen because the enzymes are gone. For soaking to work, use raw, unsalted, unroasted nuts.

How we do it

Our method

  • Place raw walnuts or almonds in a glass bowl
  • Cover completely with filtered water — use about twice the volume of water to nuts
  • Leave on the counter overnight — 6 to 12 hours for walnuts, 8 to 12 hours for almonds
  • In the morning, drain and rinse thoroughly under cold filtered water
  • Eat immediately or store in the fridge in a covered glass container for up to 2 to 3 days
  • Do not add salt to the soaking water — plain filtered water is all you need

The honest bottom line

Soaking walnuts and almonds overnight is a simple, low-effort practice with real benefits — better taste, easier digestion, and a meaningful reduction in enzyme inhibitors and tannins. The phytic acid reduction is real but modest for nuts compared to grains and legumes. The improved flavor and digestibility are consistent and noticeable.

We soak our walnuts and almonds because they taste noticeably better, our children eat them more willingly, and they feel easier to digest. Those are sufficient reasons on their own, and the traditional food cultures that developed this practice did not need clinical trials to confirm what they could observe in practice.

If you are not currently soaking your nuts, try it for a week and notice whether you can tell the difference. With walnuts in particular, the flavor improvement alone is worth the minimal effort of leaving a bowl of water on the counter overnight.
